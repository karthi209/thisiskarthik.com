The Last of Us Part 1 is video game storytelling at its best

Nov 23 · games

Spoilers for The Last of Us Part I, including the ending.

I finally finished The Last of Us Part I, including the Left Behind DLC, and I think it's easily one of the best cinematic storytelling experiences I've had in gaming.

I would put it somewhere in the same league as Ghost of Tsushima, Red Dead Redemption 2 and Uncharted 4. Probably one step below all time greats. It's not quite up there with The Witcher 3 or Bloodborne, but that's not necessarily because it does anything particularly wrong. Those games offer something very different, and I value those things a lot.

One thing I kept thinking about while playing The Last of Us is how well Naughty Dog understands what kind of experience it wants to create. It's not trying to be an enormous open-world RPG, it's not trying to give me a hundred different ways to approach everything, and it's definitely not trying to be Bloodborne, where I'm left wandering around for hours figuring out what the hell I'm supposed to do.

It's a very carefully directed experience. Almost like an interactive film, except I'm actually travelling with these characters, fighting alongside them, exploring the world and gradually becoming attached to them.

And I think that's where the game really shines.

The world and the journey

I think I enjoyed the first half of the game more than the second, particularly the autumn sections and Bill's town.

The overgrown cities, empty buildings, forests, roads, houses that people simply left behind. It's a beautiful world, even though everything about it is tragic. And the game doesn't always need to tell me what happened. You walk into a building, see the remains of someone's life, perhaps find a note, and you start imagining what those people went through. They had families, plans, ordinary lives. And now they're gone.

I particularly remember Ish's story. It's such a small part of the overall game, but it's one of those things that makes the world feel much bigger than Joel and Ellie. There were thousands of other stories happening in this world, and most of them we'll never know.

I think Naughty Dog did an exceptional job with environmental storytelling here. I enjoyed it more than what they did in Uncharted 4, although Bloodborne is still probably in another league when it comes to making the world itself part of the mystery.

What I also loved was the quietness.

Not every moment needs an infected encounter or a dramatic cutscene. Sometimes you're just walking around with Ellie, listening to her talk, exploring a place that hasn't seen normal human life in decades.

Her jokes, the occasional funny conversation, the little moments where she behaves like an ordinary teenager... these things are incredibly important. They remind you that there's still something worth living for in this miserable world. I wish there had been a little more of that in the second half.

The change in tone

The latter half of the game, especially Winter, felt considerably more oppressive to me. The story gets more serious, the stakes are increasing, and the characters are being pushed to their limits. But I think I personally enjoyed the earlier balance of exploration, tension, mystery and occasional humor more.

It's something I've noticed about my own preferences in games. I don't mind dark stories at all. Some of my favorite games are incredibly dark. But I like it when there's some relief between all that darkness.

The Witcher 3 is a great example. You can go through some absolutely horrible quests, witness terrible things, and then stumble upon some ridiculous conversation or a genuinely funny character. The world feels like it has room for everything.

The Last of Us is obviously a much more focused story, so I don't expect the same thing here.

Still, I found myself missing those earlier moments as the game progressed.

Joel and Ellie

I think the biggest reason this game works as well as it does is Joel and Ellie.

In fact, I genuinely think if these two characters had been even slightly less interesting, my rating of the game would have dropped quite significantly.

Maybe to a 4/5 or even 3.5/5.

That's how much the entire experience depends on them.

The gameplay itself isn't particularly revolutionary. The exploration is limited, the combat is fairly straightforward, and you're generally following a predetermined path.

But none of that really matters when you're invested in the people you're travelling with.

Joel is a particularly interesting character because he's not exactly a conventional hero. He's done terrible things, he's clearly carrying an enormous amount of grief, and he's not always a particularly pleasant person.

Ellie, on the other hand, brings something completely different into his life.

Watching their relationship change throughout the journey is probably the most important part of the entire game.

And I like that it doesn't happen overnight.

It's the little conversations, the arguments, the moments where they start trusting each other, and eventually the point where you realise Joel cares about Ellie far more than he's willing to admit.

By the end, I cared about both of them.

And that's what makes the ending work.

That ending...

Well, Joel certainly went on a rampage.

The entire hospital sequence is quite something. After everything they've been through, Joel finally reaches the Fireflies, only to discover that creating a cure would mean sacrificing Ellie.

And he simply refuses to accept that.

He kills his way through the hospital, kills the surgeon, rescues Ellie, and eventually kills Marlene too. 1

It's quite disturbing when you actually think about it.

From Joel's perspective, I completely understand why he does it. He's already lost his daughter, and after spending an entire year travelling with Ellie, he's effectively found someone to love and protect again.

He simply cannot go through that loss a second time.

But does that justify what he did?

That's a different question.

The Fireflies weren't exactly innocent either. They were prepared to operate on Ellie without giving her a choice, and the operation would kill her.

At the same time, Joel doesn't give Ellie that choice either.

He makes the decision for her.

And then he lies to her about what happened.

I think that's what makes the ending so good. It doesn't really give you a straightforward heroic conclusion. Joel saves someone he loves, but he also does some genuinely terrible things in the process.

And the final conversation between Joel and Ellie leaves you wondering how much she believes him.

I actually think this is a really good place to end the story.

Which brings me to another thought...

Did this really need a Part II?

After finishing Part I, my immediate reaction was that I didn't really see why the story needed a sequel.

I know Part II exists, obviously, and I haven't properly experienced it yet, so I'm not going to judge its story.

But Part I already feels complete to me.

Not complete in the sense that every question has been answered, but complete in the sense that the story has reached a meaningful conclusion.

Joel has made his choice. Ellie has her doubts. Their relationship has changed forever, even if neither of them fully acknowledges it.

I don't necessarily need to know what happens next.

Sometimes leaving things unresolved makes an ending more powerful.

Of course, maybe I'll change my mind after playing Part II. But that's how I felt immediately after finishing the first game.

Is it actually a great game, or a great story?

This is something I've been thinking about quite a bit.

I rated The Last of Us Part I a 4.5/5, which is extremely high for me. It's in the same territory as Red Dead Redemption 2, Mass Effect and Uncharted 4.

But I still wouldn't put it quite alongside The Witcher 3 or Bloodborne.

And I think the reason comes down to what I personally value in games.

Take Bloodborne, for example.

That game barely tells you anything. It drops you into this horrifying world, gives you a weapon, and essentially tells you to figure things out yourself.

I spent something like ten hours around the same lamp in Central Yharnam, dying repeatedly, trying different routes and slowly understanding how the world worked.

Nobody was holding my hand. Nobody was constantly telling me where to go.

And when I finally defeated Father Gascoigne, the satisfaction was unbelievable.

That was an experience that could only really happen in a video game.

The Witcher 3 is different again. It gives me an enormous world to explore, characters I genuinely care about, wonderfully written side quests, and the freedom to decide how I want to spend my time.

The Last of Us doesn't really offer those things to the same extent.

It's much more controlled.

But I don't think that's a criticism by itself.

Naughty Dog clearly wasn't trying to create the kind of experience FromSoftware or CD Projekt Red creates.

They wanted to tell a very specific story about two people travelling through a broken world.

And they did that extraordinarily well.

In fact, I think The Last of Us is almost perfect at being the kind of game it wants to be.

It's just that, personally, I tend to value games that also give me a greater sense of discovery, freedom and agency.

That doesn't make one approach objectively superior to the other.

It just explains why I can think The Last of Us is brilliant and still prefer something like Bloodborne.

Final thoughts

The Last of Us Part I is an exceptional experience.

The world is beautiful, the environmental storytelling is fantastic, the atmosphere is consistently convincing, and Joel and Ellie are among the most memorable characters I've encountered in gaming.

I loved the earlier sections of the journey, particularly the quieter moments where the game simply lets you exist in its world.

I wasn't quite as fond of the increasingly oppressive tone of the latter half, but I understand what the story was trying to accomplish.

And the ending is genuinely excellent.

It's uncomfortable, morally complicated, and leaves you thinking about the characters long after the credits roll.

I also appreciate that Naughty Dog didn't try to turn this into something it wasn't. It's a linear, cinematic, carefully directed experience, and it embraces that identity completely.

Would I call it one of the greatest games I've ever played?

Absolutely.

Would I put it above The Witcher 3 or Bloodborne?

No.

But would I recommend it to someone who wants to experience what video games are capable of as a storytelling medium?

Without hesitation.

My rating: 4.5/5.
